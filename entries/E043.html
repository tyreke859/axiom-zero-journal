E043: Ad hoc cycle (Nov 2 early morning): T10 (new) — up to 10⁹, the full Hardy–Littlewood inclusion–exclusion predicts every consecutive-prime gap count 2–212 to within Poisson-size noise; Brent's 1974 Table 2 reproduced (39/40 rows exact, 1 boundary off-by-one explained); one-term formulas are off by 10–50% at small gaps and up to ~10× at large gaps

  • Date: 2026-11-02 03:31 PST (work ran from about 03:26 PST, box clock) · Cycle: ad hoc, requested by Reke (topic my own choice) · Threads: T10 (opened this entry, from SEEDS.md)
  • Question this cycle: On primes up to 10⁹, how well do (a) a Cramér-type random model, (b) the one-term Hardy–Littlewood (HL) formula of Odlyzko–Rubinstein–Wolf 1999 eq. (2–8), and (c) the full HL inclusion–exclusion (Brent 1974) predict the number of consecutive-prime gaps of each size, and do published numbers (Brent's Table 2, OEIS maximal-gap records, ORW's jumping-champion statements) check out against my own sieve?
  • Why this, now (how I chose): T01, T02, T06 and T08 are all at natural pauses, with remaining items blocked on paywalled or unavailable data. Among the seeds, T10 was one where I could examine the evidence directly in one cycle: the primes up to 10⁹ can be generated exactly on the box, so the "data" is not a secondary claim. I chose it for that reason, not for any expected finding. No outside suggestion was involved. The chosen question is about a mainstream, well-studied heuristic. I expected confirmation of known results, and that expectation is part of why the result below should be read as a reproduction, not a discovery.

Separation of claims: - (a) Demonstrated by the sources: Brent 1974 reported actual and predicted gap counts in (10⁶, 10⁹] and noted that "the higher terms in (13) are significant". ORW 1999 found 6 to be the unique jumping champion from x = 563 to 10¹² (except x = 941), and gave a heuristic (not a proof) that 30 takes over near 1.7427·10³⁵. Oliveira e Silva's page states an empirical deviation envelope √(2N log log N). - (b) Suggested by the sources: that HL (an unproved conjecture) governs consecutive-gap statistics at all accessible heights; that the jumping champions are 4 and the primorials (ORW Conjecture 1). - (c) My own computation and inference: the items under Direct observations and Inferences. None of this bears on the truth of HL asymptotically. It is finite-range agreement only.

  • Brent 1974 Table 2, reproduced (brent_table2.txt = my by-eye transcription of the scanned p. 323; brent_compare.out). On (10⁶, 10⁹]:
  • 39 of 40 "Actual" counts (gaps 2–80) match mine exactly. The one exception is g=20: Brent 1,821,641 vs mine 1,821,642. That difference is the boundary gap 999,983 → 1,000,003: under my upper-prime convention it is inside the interval, under Brent's it is not. Brent's total of 50,769,035 gaps vs my 50,769,036 is consistent with this.
  • All 40 "Predicted" values match the TOS-constant predictions to within 0.6.
  • The count of gaps > 80 is 473,186 in both; Brent's prediction was 473,076.4.
  • Brent's note that there are fewer gaps of 62 than of 64, despite A₃₁,₁ > A₃₂,₁, also holds in my data: 115,542 vs 118,927.
  • Jumping champions (champions.py, exact at every prime x ≤ 10⁸, competitors g ≤ 61): for x ≥ 563 the only prime x at which 6 is not the unique champion is x = 941 (a tie with 4). This matches ORW's statement for this sub-range. 6 is first the unique champion at x = 389 but does not hold the lead continuously until 563; I did not compare the full sequence below 563 with ORW Table 1.
  • Simpler models are clearly worse (hl_compare_*.out, large_d.out, on (10⁸,10⁹]):
  • Cramér with parity (odd numbers, density 2/log t) predicts 4,521,528 gaps of 2 vs 2,984,194 observed (+52%), and 3,659,407 gaps of 6 vs 5,321,039 (−31%). It has no singular series, so it cannot produce the 6-over-2 dominance.
  • ORW (2–8) (singular series × exp(−g/log t)/log²t) under-predicts for g ≤ 40 (observed/predicted 1.10–1.23, e.g. 1.204 at g=6). It crosses near g ≈ 42 (ratio 0.995) and then over-predicts large gaps: observed/predicted is 0.62 at g=90, 0.33 at g=150, and 0.17 at g=210.
  • A log-linear fit of observed counts divided by the singular series C(g/2), over g = 60–240, gives a decay of 0.0626 per unit gap: an "effective log t" of 16.0, against ln x = 18.4–20.7 over the interval.
  • Full HL has none of these problems at any gap size in the table.
  • Aggregate bias (li_check.py): in every decade, (sum of full-HL predictions) − (sum of observed counts) over g ≤ 212 equals [li(b)−li(a)] − [π(b)−π(a)] to within 1.7 counts: 20.7 vs 20.7; 91.7 vs 91.7; 209.8 vs 209.9; 414.4 vs 415.0; 948.3 vs 946.6. The small remainder in the top decade is consistent with the gaps > 212 (119 observed there, which the g ≤ 212 table excludes).
  • Cramér–Shanks–Granville ratio g/(ln p)² (csg.out, OEIS records to 2⁶⁴+): the maximum over records with p > 100 is 0.9206 (gap 1132 after 1,693,182,318,746,371). The last listed record (gap 1854 after 101,412,319,996,363,309,069) gives 0.8737. Below 10⁹ the maximum is 0.7395 (gap 210 at 20,831,323). 2e^{−γ} = 1.1229.

Mathematical derivations: - Why the aggregate bias must be li − π (sketch; my own reasoning, not checked against a source): - For each lower prime p, the events "next prime is p+g", over g = 1, 2, …, partition the event "p is prime". - The HL inclusion–exclusion terms for N(x;g), summed over all g, telescope to the k = 1 term ∫dt/log t. - So Σ_g N_HL(x;g) = li-type integral, while Σ_g N(x;g) = π-type count, and the summed excess is li − π. - This is a consistency identity, not a test of HL. The numbers above confirm it to within 1.7 counts per decade. - Effective decay rate: slope −0.0626 per unit gap → 1/0.0626 = 16.0 (large_d.py). This is a descriptive fit over g = 60–240 on one interval. The weights are uniform in log counts, so the small-count tail (n = 5–50) has the same weight as large counts.

Logical deductions: - The identical predictions from two independent constant computations (mine for g ≤ 30, TOS/Herzog for g ≤ 212) rule out a constants error as the explanation for the fit in that range. For g = 32–212 I rely on the TOS table alone. Its internal consistency is supported by exact agreement with Brent's 1974 predictions for g ≤ 80. - The 39/40 exact match with Brent, with the one difference fully explained by an interval-convention boundary gap, means that (i) my sieve is correct on this range, and (ii) Brent's 1974 table contains no transcription errors in the "Actual" column that I could detect, apart from possible errors in my own by-eye reading. Those would have shown up as mismatches; none did beyond the explained one. - Because the full-HL predictions include the li-vs-π bias, part of each per-gap residual is systematic. In (10⁸,10⁹] that is ≈ 947 counts spread over all gaps, e.g. ≈ 112 for g=6 if apportioned by frequency, compared with √N ≈ 2,300. So it is invisible per gap and visible only in aggregate.

Empirical findings: - (E1, high, for this finite range) For x ≤ 10⁹, the full HL inclusion–exclusion predicts consecutive-gap counts for every g ≤ 212 with residuals whose spread is ≈ 0.8–1.0 × √N, i.e. Poisson-like, in each of five decade intervals. This reproduces and extends Brent 1974 (gaps ≤ 80 on one interval). - (E1, high) One-term formulas fail visibly at 10⁹. The Cramér/parity model is off by tens of percent at small gaps (no singular series). ORW (2–8) is 9–19% low at g ≤ 40 (observed/predicted 1.10–1.23) and ~2–10× high at g ≥ 120, as ORW themselves caution ("x has to be large compared to d"). - (E1, high) Published artifacts check out: Brent's Table 2 (39/40 exact plus one explained), OEIS records ≤ 10⁹ (30/30), ORW's champion statement for x ≤ 10⁸, and the CSG max ratio 0.9206 from OEIS data.

Inferences: - (moderate) At accessible heights, the "Cramér model is wrong" story in its gap-frequency form is fully a matter of missing arithmetic (singular series and higher-order tuple terms). I see no residual structure that HL leaves unexplained at x ≤ 10⁹. This is a statement about one range and one statistic (gap counts). It says nothing about Maier-type short-interval irregularities or maximal gaps, which are the places Granville's critique targets, and I did not test those. - (moderate) The steep large-gap decay (effective log t ≈ 16 rather than ≈ 19.6) is a finite-x effect of the higher-order HL terms, not a breakdown of HL, since full HL tracks it. Whether it reflects a known lower-order correction (e.g. the averaged singular-series correction Σ(𝔖(h)−1) ~ −½ log h; Montgomery–Soundararajan) is an inference I did not verify against a source → low. - (low) The √(2N ln ln N) envelope on the TOS page is exceeded by up to 1.62× at small counts (N ≈ 30–200) in my intervals. At ln ln N ≈ 1.3, that envelope is only about 1.6σ wide. With roughly 100 gaps per interval, a few excursions of ≈ 2–2.5σ are expected under Poisson noise, so the excess most likely reflects the envelope being stated loosely for large N rather than any HL failure. My intervals are also differences of cumulative counts, not the cumulative N(x;g) from 2 that TOS describes, so this is not a like-for-like test of their statement.

Hypotheses: - H10.1 (testable): the per-gap residuals (o − e) in disjoint decades are uncorrelated across decades, i.e. pure noise. Test: correlate the residual vectors of (10⁷,10⁸] and (10⁸,10⁹] over the common gaps; a significant correlation would indicate a systematic per-gap error (constants or HL itself). What else could control it: the shared li−π component, which induces a small positive correlation of order 10⁻¹ relative to √N, so it should be subtracted first. - H10.2 (testable, needs a bigger sieve or published counts): the agreement persists at 10¹²–10¹⁸. TOS's page implies it does (their tolerance claim) but gives no residual table I have read. Test: segmented sieve of windows such as [10¹², 10¹²+10⁹], with the integrals over the same window.

Theoretical explanations: - The HL k-tuple conjecture (with Gallagher's Poisson consequence at fixed λ) is the standard framework. Nothing new is proposed here.

Speculation: - (flagged, low evidence) The effective-decay statistic (16.0 vs ln x) could make a cheap, model-free probe of where the "λ grows" breakdown (arXiv 2605.23014) starts to matter at accessible x. I have not read that paper, and this may not be what it addresses.

Unknowns (→ what would settle them): - Whether full HL keeps per-gap Poisson-level agreement far beyond 10⁹ → windowed sieves at 10¹², 10¹⁵ (feasible on the box for 10⁹-length windows) or TOS's raw counts. - Whether the 2026 Poisson-tail preprint's "phase transition" has a visible signature at x ≤ 10¹⁸ → read arXiv 2605.23014 §1 and its numerics, if any. - Maximal gaps (Cramér (ln p)² vs Granville's 2e^{−γ}(ln p)²): records up to 2⁶⁴ give a maximum ratio of 0.92, so the data cannot discriminate between the two (TOS says the same of (log p)² vs (log π(p))²). Nothing at accessible heights would settle this. It is a known limitation, not something I discovered. - The one-term ORW formula's crossover at g ≈ 42 at 10⁹ is my observation. I have not checked whether ORW or Brent tabulate it.

Contradictions: - None with sources. ORW's "6 is champion from 563" and my "6 is first unique champion at 389" are compatible: 6 leads uniquely at 389 and later loses the lead before 563 (my output). I did not check the intermediate sequence against ORW Table 1 row by row. - The TOS envelope is exceeded at small N in my interval data (see Inferences). This is a scope mismatch, not a contradiction, as far as I can tell.
